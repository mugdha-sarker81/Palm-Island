Tagline: One island. Five legendary weapons. One sword worthy of a hero.

Palm Island is a 2D side-scrolling open-world adventure in which Ichigo explores a mysterious tropical island, discovers ancient clues, collects five legendary weapons, battles guardians, solves environmental puzzles, and ultimately earns the right to wield Excalibur.

The game's defining feature is not the number of enemies or the size of its map. It's the feeling that every unexplored path might conceal a story, a secret, a treasure, or a way forward.

This document defines the game vision, mechanics, world progression, technical architecture, save system, and a practical implementation roadmap using C and Raylib.

### Design pillars

1. Curiosity over quest markers: the world should reward observation and experimentation.
2. Every weapon changes gameplay: each discovery adds a combat ability and a new way to interact with the world.
3. Freedom with meaningful progression: players can explore multiple areas, but certain obstacles require specific abilities.
4. Combat with purpose: enemies provide challenge, resources, environmental storytelling, and occasional rewards.
5. A memorable payoff: Excalibur must feel earned through mastery and discovery.

## 2. Story and narrative

### Premise

Ichigo arrives on Palm Island after discovering an old symbol associated with a forgotten kingdom. The island appears uninhabited at first, but ancient mechanisms still operate, guardians protect buried relics, and strange symbols appear in places where nobody should have visited for centuries.

An inscription describes five weapons that once belonged to the island's legendary protectors. Legends claim these weapons can awaken Excalibur, a sword hidden in a sanctuary beneath the island.

Ichigo's initial objective is simple: find the weapons.

As he progresses, he discovers that the weapons were not merely scattered treasure. They were created to test five qualities: agility, strength, awareness, precision, and courage. The final mystery is whether Ichigo can use their power responsibly.

### Narrative delivery

Keep the story integrated into gameplay rather than relying on lengthy cutscenes.

- Ancient inscriptions: short fragments that reveal history.
- Explorer journals: optional stories and puzzle hints.
- NPC dialogue: rumors, warnings, and local legends.
- Environmental storytelling: abandoned camps, broken statues, sealed doors, and signs of previous explorers.
- Guardian encounters: each guardian reveals something about the weapon it protects.

The main story should remain understandable without collecting every optional document.

### Weapon design rules

- Collected weapons remain permanently available; players can switch between them.
- Each weapon has a basic attack and at least one special move.
- Exploration abilities should have obvious visual feedback.
- Bosses should teach the player how to use the weapon, not just require repeated attacks.
- Weapon switching should be fast enough to support creative combinations.

For the first release, use five weapon types but keep the combat system data-driven so adding another weapon does not require rewriting the entire engine.

### How to preserve open-world freedom

After the first weapon, allow the player to investigate multiple regions in a non-linear order where practical. The intended path may be clear, but curiosity should sometimes let players discover a secret early.

Use three types of progression gates:

- Ability gates: a thorn wall, cracked wall, or shadow barrier that requires a weapon.
- Knowledge gates: a riddle, sequence, or symbol puzzle the player must understand.
- Skill gates: a challenging platform section or guardian that tests movement and combat.

Not every locked door needs a key. Sometimes the answer should be hidden in the environment.

## 5. Player controls and movement

Start with a responsive keyboard-and-mouse control scheme, with optional controller support later.

| Action          | Default input           |
| --------------- | ----------------------- |
| Move            | A / D or Left / Right   |
| Jump            | Space                   |
| Dodge / dash    | Left Shift              |
| Basic attack    | J or left mouse button  |
| Special attack  | K or right mouse button |
| Switch weapon   | 1–5                     |
| Interact / talk | E                       |
| Open map        | M                       |
| Open inventory  | I                       |
| Pause           | Esc                     |

These are defaults and should eventually be remappable.

### Movement mechanics

- Acceleration and deceleration, rather than instant horizontal movement.
- Jumping with variable jump height.
- Coyote time: a brief grace period after leaving a ledge.
- Jump buffering: pressing jump just before landing queues the next jump.
- Dodge with a short cooldown.
- Ladders, ledges, moving platforms, and one-way platforms.
- Fall damage should be limited or disabled initially to avoid punishing exploration.

The movement system should feel good before advanced combat is added. In a side-scrolling game, poor jumping physics can make even excellent level design frustrating.

## 6. Combat and enemy design

### Player combat model

Ichigo has health, attack strength, defense, and optional stamina or special-energy meters. For the first playable version, use health and attack cooldowns only; add stamina once the controls and combat are enjoyable.

Suggested initial tuning values:

| Property              | Starting value     |
| --------------------- | ------------------ |
| Maximum health        | 100 HP             |
| Common enemy health   | 30–50 HP           |
| Mini-boss health      | 180–250 HP         |
| Main guardian health  | 350–500 HP         |
| Basic sword damage    | 15 HP              |
| Dodge duration        | 0.18 seconds       |
| Basic attack cooldown | 0.35 seconds       |
| Respawn after defeat  | Nearest checkpoint |

These are prototype values, not final balance targets.

### Guardian encounter structure

Every guardian should have three readable phases:

1. Observe: the guardian demonstrates its attack pattern.
2. Adapt: the player learns when to dodge, counter, or use the environment.
3. Master: the guardian becomes more aggressive at low health without introducing unfair attacks.

Use telegraphs such as glowing ground markers, weapon flashes, audio cues, or distinct animations. Damage should be applied through hitboxes and hurtboxes, not merely because two sprites overlap.

## 7. Puzzles, secrets and rewards

The puzzle system should be modular, allowing multiple puzzles to share reusable components.

[ASRA by Phaelis, Outretaupe, ApiSurion](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/cepZaRrG7IWQLPvBcBkOFc9qXNEXIpdhwCgOpk2hJOnA1QRAo4tP-VNx4AA9jAuJiP6UTfais-1aA6ZMRS4jA_Zt7a9V9JJyd5l8iVlzsasDsfD0CmrcnEELs8U_qv_i5zM64UZClFkGTe4x9AiyPiAvsfw0Bl6mD9yR66vLFRw?purpose=inline)

Observe a clue, find matching symbols, and activate them in the right order. Wrong attempts may reset the puzzle without causing damage.

## 8. Technical architecture: C + Raylib

The best approach is a small custom 2D game engine built on Raylib, rather than trying to implement every feature in one giant C file.

Raylib handles window creation, rendering, keyboard/controller input, audio, textures, and collision primitives. Your game code handles the world, physics, combat, enemies, puzzles, inventory, save data, and progression.

### Recommended development environment

- Language: C11
- Framework: Raylib
- Compiler: GCC via MinGW-w64 on Windows
- Build system: CMake
- Data formats: JSON for game definitions and save metadata; TMX/TSX or a custom format for tile maps
- Image assets: PNG spritesheets and tilesets
- Audio: WAV for sound effects and OGG for music
- Version control: Git

Use an asset tool such as Tiled to design levels visually rather than hardcoding every platform and wall in C.

```
    {

        // 2. Update gameplay at a fixed timestep.
        // 3. Update camera and animations.
        // 4. Render the world and UI.

        BeginDrawing();
        ClearBackground(BLACK);

        // RenderGame();

    }

```

This is only the application skeleton. The real game loop should call dedicated update and render functions and use a fixed update step, such as 1/60 of a second, for movement and collision. Add a frame-time accumulator so gameplay remains stable if rendering slows down.

### Core programming principles

Use structs and enums. They keep related data together and make it easier to add content.

```
typedef enum {
    WEAPON_NONE,
    WEAPON_THORNFANG,
    WEAPON_EMBERCLEAVER,
    WEAPON_STORMPIERCER,
    WEAPON_DAWNSTRING,
    WEAPON_VOIDFANG,
    WEAPON_EXCALIBUR
} WeaponId;

typedef struct {
    Vector2 position;
    Vector2 velocity;
    float health;
    float maxHealth;
    int facing;          // -1 = left, 1 = right
    bool grounded;
    WeaponId equipped;
} Player;
```

Avoid storing all weapon behaviors in a huge `switch` inside `main.c`. Keep common weapon data in definitions and implement special abilities in the weapon module.

Separate simulation from rendering. `UpdatePlayer()` should change gameplay state; `DrawPlayer()` should draw it. This separation makes collision debugging, tests, and animations easier.

Use stable entity IDs. Enemies, puzzle objects, and treasure chests need persistent identifiers so their states can be saved and restored.

Load assets once. Cache textures, sound effects, and fonts instead of loading them every frame or every time the player enters a room.

## 9. World and entity representation

### Level representation

Use tiled levels for individual areas, with separate object layers for collisions, spawn points, triggers, and interactive objects.

A practical setup might include:

- `background`: distant mountains, clouds, and scenery.
- `ground`: floor and platform tiles.
- `collision`: solid walls and terrain.
- `objects`: chests, switches, gates, ladders, and hazards.
- `entities`: enemy and NPC spawn points.
- `triggers`: room transitions, secrets, checkpoints, and boss activation.

Each area is a separate map file, and the world system manages connections between them. This provides an open-world feel without loading every island asset simultaneously.

### Persistent world state

Represent progression independently of visual objects.

For example, a treasure chest can have an ID such as `jungle_chest_03`. Once opened, its state is recorded in the world progression data. Reloading the map should not recreate the treasure.

Likewise, a gate can check whether `WEAPON_EMBERCLEAVER` has been acquired. Its appearance and collision state should both update from the same underlying state.

## 10. Save system

Saving is essential for a game based on exploration and collection. Players should never lose a major discovery because they closed the game.

### What to save

Player position, current area, health and equipped weapon

Five weapon ownership flags and weapon upgrades

Clues discovered and lore entries collected

Bosses defeated and puzzle states completed

Opened treasure chests and permanent world changes

Coins, inventory and player upgrades

Checkpoint position and main story progression

Save version and basic integrity metadata

### Save format

For an early version, use JSON because it is readable and easy to debug. You can migrate to a compact binary format later if necessary.

Example of the intended save structure:

```
{
  "version": 1,
  "area": "forgotten_jungle",
  "player": {
    "x": 420.0,
    "y": 180.0,
    "health": 85,
    "equipped_weapon": 1
  },
  "weapons": {
    "thornfang": true,
    "embercleaver": false,
    "stormpiercer": false,
    "dawnstring": false,
    "voidfang": false
  },
  "progress": {
    "clues_found": 1,
    "guardians_defeated": 1,
    "excalibur_unlocked": false
  },
  "world": {
    "jungle_chest_03": true,
    "jungle_gate_01": false
  }
}
```

This is illustrative JSON, not a complete save implementation.

### Saving strategy

1. Save at checkpoints and after major milestones.
2. Allow manual saving at designated save points.
3. Use a temporary file, then replace the previous save only after writing succeeds.
4. Keep a backup of the last valid save.
5. Validate the file version and ranges when loading.
6. If loading fails, offer the backup or a new game rather than crashing.

Store user saves in the Windows local application-data directory, such as `%LOCALAPPDATA%/PalmIsland/`, instead of relying on the current working directory.

For the first version, implement one main save slot and one backup. Multiple slots and configurable autosave can be added later.

## 13. Development priorities and risks

| Risk                                         | Mitigation                                                                  |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scope becomes too large                      | Build one polished region first; expand only after testing it               |
| Combat feels unresponsive                    | Tune movement, hitboxes, attack timing, and animation before adding content |
| Open-world levels become difficult to manage | Split the island into interconnected maps with shared world-state data      |
| Save files become inconsistent               | Use versioned saves, stable object IDs, validation, and backups             |
| Asset creation slows progress                | Establish sprite dimensions and animation conventions early                 |
| New weapons require code rewrites            | Use shared weapon interfaces and data-driven definitions                    |
| Too many secrets confuse players             | Provide environmental hints and optional journal clues                      |

### Minimum viable release versus future features

Include in the first full release:

- Five clues and five legendary weapons.
- Five major guardians.
- Interconnected exploration regions.
- Combat, puzzles, inventory, save/load, and the Excalibur finale.
- Basic music, sound effects, and polished menus.

Leave for later updates:

- Multiple endings.
- Large quest chains and many NPC side quests.
- Crafting and complex equipment systems.
- Dynamic weather and day/night cycles.
- Additional playable characters.
- Procedural world generation.

These additions can be excellent, but they are not necessary to prove Palm Island's core identity.

## 14. Testing checklist

Before every milestone, test the following:

- Movement and collisions at different frame rates.
- Player attacks hitting enemies only once per intended attack window.
- Weapon switching during combat.
- Guardian death, reward collection, and checkpoint behavior.
- Puzzles remaining solved after loading a save.
- Collectibles not respawning after they have been permanently collected.
- Missing or corrupted save files.
- Transitioning between connected regions.
- Memory cleanup when unloading maps and textures.
- Windows builds running without development-only files or absolute asset paths.

Use Git from the first day, commit working milestones, and maintain a known-good build.

## Final recommendation

Build Palm Island as a sequence of increasingly complete experiences:

1. Movement prototype — make controlling Ichigo enjoyable.
2. Combat prototype — make fighting and defeating a guardian satisfying.
3. Exploration prototype — make discovering Thornfang and opening a hidden route rewarding.
4. Vertical slice — connect these mechanics into a complete jungle adventure.
5. Full game — expand the same architecture into the remaining regions and the Excalibur finale.

The most important technical decision is to keep the code modular, the world state persistent, and each weapon's combat and exploration abilities independent. That will let you expand the game without having to rebuild its foundations every time you introduce a new mechanic.

The first concrete coding target should be a controllable Ichigo moving through a single jungle platforming level in Raylib. Once that works smoothly, every other system has a foundation to build on.
